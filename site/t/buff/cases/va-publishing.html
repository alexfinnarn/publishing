Case study · CivicActions · 2022–2024

Accelerated publishing at the VA

A federal content platform where getting a change in front of veterans was slower than deciding to make it. The fix was decoupling — but the reason it worked was workflow, not architecture.

Role Senior Backend Drupal Engineer
Client U.S. Dept. of Veterans Affairs
Stack Drupal, JSON:API, Next.js / React

The situation

Federal publishing has constraints most commercial CMS work does not. Accessibility is not a nice-to-have, it is statute. Review chains are real and they are not going away. And the audience is people who need an answer today, from an institution they may already find hard to deal with.

The platform itself was Drupal, and Drupal was the right call. The problem was the distance between an editor deciding something and a reader seeing it.

What I did

The work was decoupling the read path: Drupal stayed the place content is authored and governed, JSON:API became the contract, and a Next/React front end took over delivery. That is a common enough shape. The part that took judgment was deciding what not to decouple, because every seam you add is a seam that can break at 4pm on a Friday before a holiday weekend.

Alongside it, on the same platform: features across roughly fifteen Drupal sites, and a Drupal-to-Drupal migration built on the Migrate API.

On federal detail

I keep public descriptions of government work deliberately short. What is above is accurate and checkable; the specifics of programs, timelines, and internal process I will talk through directly rather than publish. Ask me.

What I took from it

Decoupling gets sold as a performance decision. In practice it is an organizational one — you are choosing which team can move without waiting for which other team. If the new architecture does not shorten that wait, you have added infrastructure and changed nothing that matters. I wrote about the general version of this in Intent over Implementation.
