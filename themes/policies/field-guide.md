# Field Guide — v0.1

Status: provisional policy for the first abstract theme experiment.
Use with the shared [application process](../PROCESS.md).

## Interpretation

**F1 — Identify a recognizable situation.** Find the situation, recurring
problem, or phenomenon the material helps a reader understand. Select by
relevance to the communication brief, then source support; use source order
to break remaining ties and record ambiguity.

Identify supplied recognition cues, possible responses, and limits. A single
case is a case, not proof of a universal pattern. If the material supports no
generalization, frame the guide as orientation to that specific case.

## Composition

**F2 — Build a path from recognition to response.** Open with the situation
and scope. Group material into recognition cues, supported actions or
responses, and limitations. Use descriptive headings that help a reader find
the part they need. Preserve required dependencies and sequences.

Use numbered steps only for a supported sequence. Present alternatives as
alternatives. Keep conditions and cautions beside the response they constrain.
If the input contains no actions, produce a recognition guide and explicitly
state that the material does not establish what action to take.

**F3 — Favor usable distinctions.** Give space to differences that help the
reader recognize the situation or understand when a response applies. Compress
background only when doing so preserves those distinctions. Record omissions;
required inclusions and important exceptions survive.

## Expression

**F4 — Explain without overstating advice.** Use concrete descriptions of what
a reader might observe. Use direct instructions only when the source supports
prescriptive guidance. Describe historical responses as what participants did,
not what every reader should do. Explain terms from supplied context.

Rewrite and summarize when helpful, preserving marked quotations. Do not
invent symptoms, diagnostic criteria, best practices, or missing steps to make
the guide appear useful. Mark plausible but unsupported interpretations as
questions in the decision record, not instructions in the publication.

## Presentation

**F5 — Support lookup and return visits.** Favor clear section labels, compact
but readable groups, and visible connections between cues, responses, and
limits. Use lists when items are parallel, sequences when order matters, and
comparison structures only when material actually shares comparison dimensions.

At narrower sizes, groups may stack but labels and relationships remain clear.
Use relative sizing and comfortable reading measures. Do not hide limitations
in small print, rely only on color, or force every section into an identical box.

## Boundaries and acceptance

**F6 — Separate observation from prescription.** A theme may reorganize an
account for recognition and lookup; it may not upgrade an anecdote into advice
or evidence of effectiveness. Apply the shared source-preservation checks.

Check the resolved publication:

- Is the situation and its scope clear at the beginning?
- Can a reader find the supported recognition cues?
- Are actions distinguished from historical examples and unsupported possibilities?
- Are sequences, conditions, and exceptions preserved beside the relevant material?
- Can the reader tell what the material does not establish?

Missing actions produce a partial application: a recognition guide, with the
limitation stated. If even recognition cues are absent, provide a scoped
orientation to the subject and record that the policy is a poor fit for this
input. Do not pad it into a complete-looking guide.
