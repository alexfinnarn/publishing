# Career Changers Are a Workforce Pipeline Too

Ask manufacturers in the Dayton region what limits their growth and the answer is rarely demand. The region counts more than 2,500 manufacturers, roughly 130,000 manufacturing workers, and about $22.5 billion in annual product, with average earnings near $90,000. What it also counts is roughly 1,830 manufacturing job openings in an average month. In the Dayton Region Manufacturers Association's member survey, workforce is the top issue — ahead of tariffs, ahead of costs. The work is here. The people, in the usual places, are not.

## The pipelines everyone is already fishing

The region's established pipelines are good ones. High school and career-technical programs introduce students to manufacturing early. Community colleges train technicians and technologists. WorkAdvance, the recruiting and training initiative run through the region's manufacturing sector partnership, connects adults to manufacturing careers and helps them complete. Every one of these deserves support, and every manufacturer in the region is drawing from them at the same time. They are also slow by nature: a student pipeline takes years to widen, and a credential program graduates cohorts, not individuals on demand. A plant that needs a systems technician this quarter cannot wait for next year's class.

## The pool that does not apply

There is a second pool, and it rarely shows up in an applicant tracking system: experienced workers in adjacent industries. Enterprise software staff who keep business systems running around the clock. Logistics and distribution technology teams. Military technicians separating from service. Utility and industrial software people. These workers already know production discipline: uptime matters, changes get tested, the pager is real, and a system that stops costs money by the minute.

They do not apply, mostly because postings tell them not to. A bachelor's degree requirement in a specific field. Five years on one named platform. A keyword list written for a person who barely exists in this labor market. Screening software then does exactly what it was told to do, and the adjacent candidate never reaches a human who might recognize what transfers.

## The supply is about to grow

This pool is about to get larger, and employers should be ready for it. Artificial intelligence is displacing experienced white-collar workers — software and information technology staff among the first — and most of them do not want to start over. A developer with fifteen years of production experience who changes industries wants to migrate with that experience, not return to a classroom for a four-year degree in a new field. They should be able to. What a career changer needs is not a new foundation; it is a bridge: orientation to a new industry's context, its safety culture, and one or two job-specific credentials. That is a matter of weeks, not semesters — and it is a bridge an employer can help build.

The honest calibration matters here. Nobody becomes a controls engineer over a weekend, and the skilled trades have real gates, described below. But on the software side of a modern plant — the manufacturing execution systems, the SCADA-adjacent applications, the plant network, the data that moves between the floor and the business — the distance from enterprise IT is mostly context, not capability. The systems are different. The discipline is the same.

## What is actually required, and what only looks required

Some requirements in manufacturing are law or close to it, and no hiring manager can waive them. Electrical work carries state licensing. Federal safety rules govern lockout/tagout and work around energized equipment. Defense work can carry citizenship requirements under export-control law. Regulated sectors add their own: reliability standards in bulk power, validation rules in pharmaceutical production. Employers are right to hold these lines, and career changers should expect to meet them.

But a large share of what screens out adjacent candidates is not in that category. On the information side of manufacturing, there is no license to hold and no statute requiring a certificate. A degree requirement, a named-platform requirement, a years-on-this-stack requirement — these are conventions an employer chose, which means an employer can revisit them. Distinguishing the gates that are real from the gates that are habitual is the cheapest workforce intervention available: it costs nothing but an afternoon with your own job postings.

## Widening the pipeline in practice

Employers who are filling these roles are doing a few specific things:

- **Splitting the job into day-one and trainable.** For each role, list what a person genuinely needs on their first day, and what a competent hire can learn in sixty to ninety days of structured onboarding. Post the first list. Turn the second list into the training plan.
- **Using the state's bridge money.** Ohio's TechCred program reimburses employers up to $1,000 per employee, per credential — up to $30,000 per application period — for short-term, industry-recognized, technology-focused credentials. The program runs in recurring application rounds, with current dates and the eligible credential list posted at techcred.ohio.gov. It is designed for exactly this: an experienced worker who needs one credential to cross over.
- **Recruiting through the shared vehicle.** A single plant recruiting alone competes with every other plant. The sector partnership's WorkAdvance initiative exists so the region recruits and prepares people together, and it is built to include adults changing careers, not only new graduates.
- **Letting people prove it.** Trial arrangements, contract-to-hire, and structured first projects reveal more about an adjacent candidate than any interview screen — and they lower the risk that makes hiring managers cling to perfect-on-paper requirements.

## The wider pipeline

Manufacturers in this region already know how to turn motivated people into skilled workers; it is the industry's oldest habit, from apprenticeships onward. The career changers arriving now — pushed by AI, pulled by the chance to build real things — are the same opportunity at a different age, carrying experience that took years to build and asking only for a short bridge into this industry. The pipeline is wider than the postings suggest. It is worth posting like it.
